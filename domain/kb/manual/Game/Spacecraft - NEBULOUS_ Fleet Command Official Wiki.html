Spacecraft (or craft for short) are aircraft-like small vehicles that can be launched from hangars on larger ships. They can perform a number of roles, including scouting, electronic warfare, anti-missile and anti-craft fire, and anti-ship strikes. Their ability to receive orders after launch makes them more flexible than other tools such as cruise missiles.


Spacecraft Mechanics

Controls

Right-Click Commands

Craft are controlled similarly to ships, but with context-sensitive actions depending on the item the cursor is pointing at when right-clicked. Where multiple valid controls exist, additional commands may be accessed by holding Shift.

How facing changes the return

  • Ships use a fixed table of 162 directions. Their displayed signature is the maximum, and most angles are lower.
  • Craft and missiles use a radar box fixed to the airframe. Their radar cross-section (RCS) is the area of that box as the radar sees it: each face counts by how squarely it faces the radar.[4]
  • The return is smallest nose-on or tail-on.
  • It is largest when the radar sees the side, top and nose at once, for example with the craft banked 45°. That happens about 57–63° off the nose for craft, and 65–75° for missiles.
  • In level flight the largest return is only 1.24–1.35× the side-on value for craft, and 1.07–1.20× for missiles.
  • The displayed craft signature is the side-on value.
    • For craft, the banked peak is 1.6–1.7× the displayed value, and nose-on is 0.73–0.91×.
    • For missiles, the banked peak is about 1.5× the side-on value, and nose-on is 0.38–0.66×.
  • Pitch, yaw and roll all change the return, because the box turns with the airframe.
  • The same value is used by search radars on ships and craft, by track accuracy, and by missile seekers. A ship holding a fire-control lock reuses the last value computed for that target.
  • Detection range grows with the fourth root of the RCS: doubling the RCS gives about 19% more range.
    • A craft seen nose-on is detected at 0.82–0.86× the range of its peak angle.
    • A missile seen nose-on is detected at 0.71–0.81× that range.

Craft countermeasures

A pattern is a string of chaff packets dropped from under a craft as it flies. The packets form one decoy cloud that traces the craft's path. Every craft has a Countermeasures socket that holds one of three launchers.

The chaff cloud

  • Packets drop 8 m below the craft and stay where they were dropped.
  • The cloud is one box around all live packets, padded by 10 m.
    • Length = craft speed × 2.25 s + 10 m (2.375 s for High-Coherence).
    • Thickness is at least 10 m. Craft jink while evading, which makes it thicker.
  • The cloud is full size from the last packet until 6 s. After 6 s it shrinks from the oldest end. It is gone at 8.25 s (8.375 s for High-Coherence).
  • The box is aligned to the map axes, not to the flight path. A trail flown diagonally across the map makes a larger box.
  • Its radar return is the area of the box as the radar sees it, times the multiplier. Seen side-on, a cloud returns its length × 10 m. Seen end-on, it returns at least 100 m² (200 m² for High-Coherence).
  • Its wake can be seen from all directions. A craft's own wake is strength 1 before modifiers and can only be seen within 75° of its tail.
  • It fools active radar, semi-active radar and wake seekers.
  • It does nothing against EO (electro-optical), passive radar or home-on-jam seekers. Missiles with an EO validator reject it.
  • It is not flagged as a point-defense target, so seekers set to ignore small targets still see it.
  • It cannot be shot down.
  • Pattern counts refill when the craft is relaunched.

When craft drop countermeasures

  • Craft drop countermeasures only while evading. An evasion starts when a missile is 5 s or less from impact (3 s or less with DFTAC OUT-G).
  • Each evasion drops at most one pattern, at its start. An evasion lasts up to 6 s.
  • When a different threat becomes the most urgent, a new evasion starts and drops a new pattern. A staggered salvo can use up several patterns.
  • A new pattern can't start until 2.5 s after the previous one began.
  • Craft know the incoming missile's seeker types without needing intel on it.

Evasion manoeuvres:

  • Notch: fly straight away from the missile for 1.5 s (2.5 s when the Halberd, Barracuda, Pike or Tanto notches against a seeker that is neither radar nor wake), then turn across its path, presenting a side profile in the hopes that seduced missiles will be unable to re-acquire[5]. The pattern drops at the start, while the craft is still flying away, so the trail is usually not side-on to the missile.
  • Scoot: turn onto a new, partly random course. The code tilts the direction to the missile by angles fixed to the map, so the course is not at a set angle to the missile.
  • Flee: fly straight away from the threat.
  • Jink: turn hard across the line to the threat. DFTAC OUT-G uses this against missiles.
  • Sweeping turn: turn around to face an attacker behind the craft.

Against gunfire from enemy craft:

  • The Halberd, Barracuda and Pike jink when two or more enemy guns are firing at them and the fire is 1 s from impact. Two guns on one enemy craft count as two.
  • The Tanto turns to face an attacker behind it under the same conditions, and its flight-mates are ordered to attack the shooter.
  • The Claymore, Sturgeon and Sundial flee when gunfire is 5 s from impact.
  • The same gun can't trigger another evasion until 12 s after the last one started.
  • None of these drop a pattern unless CMSR is set to ALL.

High-Coherence and proximity fuzes

  • The High-Coherence cloud is solid to missile proximity fuzes.
  • A missile with a blast-fragmentation warhead has a 33% chance to detonate on the cloud each time its fuze touches it.
  • The only stock missile with an older-style proximity warhead, the FBU-15 bomb, is set to detonate on ships only, so it ignores the cloud.
  • Flak shells don't detect the cloud.
  • Standard and High-Capacity clouds don't set off fuzes.

Other craft decoys

  • Missile Decoy Projector (B-86 Claymore, Support, 10 pts)
    • Fires when the Claymore launches a missile at a ship.
    • Launches 3 decoys, 0.25 s apart, toward the target inside a 3° cone, at 1.04× the speed of the missile they cover.
    • 2 patterns per sortie, with a 5 s reset after each.
    • Each decoy has 15 HP and flies for up to 20 s. Its radar box is a 100 m cube at ×2.5 return, about 25,000 m² seen face-on.
    • Each decoy is flagged as both a point-defense target and a decoy. Enemy PD turrets give priority to decoys flying straight at them. Defensive missiles never engage decoys. Seekers set to ignore small targets ignore them.
  • Sprinter Masquerade (ANS RS-35 Halberd) and Ferryman Masquerade (OSP RS-35A Halberd)
    • Mission package, 15 pts, mass 4.
    • The craft shows on enemy sensors as a Sprinter corvette or a Ferryman clipper until it is identified.

Launch and Recovery Sequence

Select Armaments -> Give Flight Sortie Command -> Arm on a pad or strikedown slot -> Launch -> pad stays busy for Deck Traversal Time -> Return -> Land -> Disarm while that pad spends its traversal time -> Stored -> Repair if a repair slot is free

Arming/disarming time

A Craft must be readied before it can sortie from a carrier. This includes loading fuel and ordinance onto the Craft. The duration to complete the readying operation depends on the the size and ammunition loaded the time that. This ranges from arming a vehicle without armaments with virtually little to no time to a significant amount of time such as your bombers.

When a craft returns, any unspent ordinance must be removed and returned to stores, this occurs before the craft is repaired.

Some Carriers have bonuses to Arming Speed, and the Gear Deck Locker can increase the speed as well.

Deck Traversal Time

Deck Traversal Time is a pad turnaround. After a craft launches, or after it is recovered and stored, that pad cannot take another craft until the traversal time has elapsed. It does not delay the craft that is launching.

Recovering a craft also ties up the pad for the deck traversal time (after a short close delay). The craft is stored and starts disarming while that pad wait is still running.

Some Carriers have bonuses to Arming Speed, and the Flight Deck Traversal System can increase the speed as well.

Craft Repair

Damaged craft are repaired after disarming, one per free repair slot. A craft that is repairing, or still waiting for a slot, can still be launched, and it sorties with whatever damage it still has.

The number of slots that a craft can repair at is equal to the number of Spacecraft Repair Slots on the selected Hull. Carrier Hulls like the Levy, Moorline and Journeyman each have 1 slot and can only repair one craft at a time. Non-carrier hulls have no slots and cannot repair craft by default.

Each Spacecraft Repair Station adds one repair slot on top of the hull base. For example, a Marauder with 1 Spacecraft Repair Station can repair 1 Barracuda Strike Fighter at a time.

Preflight Slots

The number of slots that a carrier can arm craft is equal to the number of small and large launch pads on the selected Carrier. For example the Levy has 2 Large and 4 small pads, and the Journeyman 2 Large and 2 Small pads, while the number of pads for the Moorline is directly related to the number of Bank Hangar's installed. Both the Journeyman and Moorline can install additional small mounted hangars for additional size 1 preflight slots.

This number can be increased with Spacecraft Strikedown Stations, this will allow additional craft to be armed in addition to the ones that can be armed on the launch pads. Craft armed there move onto a launch pad as soon as one is free, which is after the previous launch or recovery finishes its deck-traversal wait, and they keep the arming progress already made. For example, a Levy or Journeyman with 2 Spacecraft Strikedown Stations can arm a 4 wing Claymore Torpedo Flight at the same time and launch them much faster than they would otherwise.
